Reader Type

Prototype Books Web App Vanilla JS
Open app

I read a lot, and I am bad at picking the next book. The recommendations I get are all built the same way: they look at what I have already read and offer me more of it. That works until you want something genuinely different, at which point being told "readers who liked this also liked" is no help at all. It can only point back at where you have already been.

Reader Type takes the other route. It asks about you, not about your bookshelf. Twenty four short statements, answered on a five point agree scale, build a profile across ten dimensions. That profile is matched against sixteen genres, and you get a genre, three books chosen to suit you, and the two next closest genres with their own shelves.

It takes about three minutes, there is no sign up, and nothing is uploaded.

What it measures

Six of the ten dimensions sit close to the Big Five personality factors: openness, structure, sociability, empathy, intensity, and curiosity. Curiosity is nearer to what psychologists call need for cognition, which predicts appetite for nonfiction better than any Big Five factor does.

The other four are about reading rather than personality, and they are the ones that do the real work:

  • Ambiguity. How comfortable you are with something that is never explained. This is the difference between science fiction, which explains its strangeness, and magical realism, which refuses to.
  • Hope. Whether you need it to come out okay in the end.
  • Humor. Whether you want the book to be funny, whatever else it is doing.
  • Factual. Whether you would rather read the invented version or the real one.

Why hope gets its own axis

Hope is the filter I think readers actually apply and quizzes never ask about. It is not the same as how intense you like things, and treating the two as one thing is why recommendations so often miss.

Never Let Me Go and Project Hail Mary are both science fiction, both well liked, and both about someone trying to solve an impossible situation. They are opposite answers to the question of whether it comes out okay. Handing the wrong one to someone is a bigger miss than getting the genre wrong, because they will put it down for reasons that have nothing to do with the subject.

How the matching works

Each genre carries its own profile across the same ten dimensions. Matching compares the shape of your profile to the shape of each genre's, rather than the raw numbers, so someone who answers strongly across the board does not simply match everything.

Two adjustments keep it honest. Both sides are standardised first: you against the range of scores the quiz can actually produce, and each genre against the other fifteen, so "high curiosity" means high relative to the other genres rather than high in the abstract. Then every genre is calibrated against how it scores for someone answering at random, which stops genres that sit in crowded parts of the space from winning by default. Without that second step the catalogue does more of the answering than the person does.

The statements are also balanced in both directions. Every dimension has as much weight on statements where agreeing raises it as on statements where agreeing lowers it, so agreeing with everything produces no result rather than a result. That is standard practice in real inventories, and without it the quiz measures how agreeable you are instead of what you like.

The books are picked for you, not for the bucket

Getting the genre right is only half of it. Literary Fiction holds both Gilead and Never Let Me Go, and telling two very different readers the same three titles because they landed in the same bucket wastes most of what the quiz just learned about them.

So every book carries two tags of its own: how hopeful it is, and how much work it asks of you. The three books you see are ordered against your own scores on those, with hope weighted more heavily. A reader who said plainly that they need things to end well gets Gilead first. A reader drawn to sad stories gets Never Let Me Go first. Same genre, different shelf.

It tells you when it is unsure

Any quiz can name a winner. Not every set of answers supports one. If your answers sat near the middle, or the top two genres are separated by almost nothing, the result page says so and points you at all three rather than presenting a coin toss as a reading of you.

You can also tell it that it got you wrong. The result page asks, and if you disagree it asks which genre would have been right. That is recorded with your profile and what it guessed, so the genre model can eventually be fitted to real responses instead of resting on my judgement. It stays in your browser, and you can export or delete it.

What it does

  • Reads the person, not the shelf. Twenty four statements about how you handle uncertainty, whether you take on other people's moods, and what you want right now. Only a few mention books at all.
  • Sixteen genres. Including the ones most quizzes flatten together, like speculative fiction and magical realism, or narrative history and historical fiction.
  • Three books, ordered for you. Chosen from the matched genre by how hopeful and how demanding each one is, against your own answers.
  • Both runners up open up. Each shows its own three books, because plenty of readers sit between two genres rather than inside one.
  • Mood asked separately. A few statements ask what you want this month rather than in general, since the two disagree more often than a personality model admits.
  • A profile you can read. All ten dimensions are shown with your score, so you can see what drove the result.
  • Neutral is a real answer. If a statement does not resonate, say so, and it counts for nothing rather than forcing a guess.
  • Light and dark, and the keyboard. Answer with the number keys, go back with backspace.

How it's built

Plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. No framework, no build step, no backend, no dependencies. The whole model lives in one data file: the ten dimensions, the twenty four statements with their weights, and the sixteen genres with their profiles and books. Changing what the quiz believes means editing that file and nothing else.

Everything runs in your browser. There is no account and nothing is sent anywhere, so your answers and any feedback you leave never leave your machine.

The honest caveat

This is entertainment with a real model underneath, not a validated instrument. The statements borrow the style of Big Five items but are not the validated scales, and every genre profile is a considered judgement call rather than something fitted to data. The feedback capture exists to change that, but until enough real responses come in, those numbers are informed guesses.

One thing I was careful about: no genre is adjusted to make particular people get particular results. Where a genre comes up too often or too rarely, the fix belongs in a rule that treats all sixteen the same, never in a nudge toward the answer someone wanted. Treat the result as a decent suggestion, not a verdict.

Try it yourself

Twenty four statements, about three minutes. Press 1 to 5 rather than clicking if you want to move quickly, and use Neutral freely. Some statements are deliberately worded so that agreeing means less of something, so read each one on its own terms.
